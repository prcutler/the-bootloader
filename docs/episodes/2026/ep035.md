## Show Notes

### USB-C cable and PD testing (Tod #1)

USB-C and Power Delivery is confusing.  Let's find some tools for that.

You know how much I'm into inspecting USB stuff. When doing any kind of embeddeded
development, I always have some kind of "USB Tester" gadget in between my computer
and the board I'm using.  I'm sure you've seen them. They show the voltage being
put out by the USB host and the current being used by the device. I've been using
them for over a decade.  Great things. Only about $10.  But they're only really
for the old USB 2.0 devices.

With the advent of USB-C, there is now one connector that can mean many things. 
This is especially true in terms of power delivery.  USB-PD is a power delivery
spec that runs over USB-C cables, where two devices negotiate for not only 
who is providing the power, but if that power is anywhere between 5V @ 3A to 
20V @ 5A. This is how your laptop can be charged by the wall but also charge
your phone, from the same port! 

And then there's cables.  Not all USB-C cables are created equal.  Many are just
USB 2.0 cables with USB-C ends. These are usually cheap. Some cables are capable
or running Thunderbolt or USB4 over them. These cables are expensive. But how to know?
You can potentially depend on the markings or the brand, but not always. The better
cables are supposed to have an e-Marker chip built into them, which reports their
abilities. But not always. 

Thankfully there are some tools out there (some hardware, some software), 
to give you insight into what's going on.  On the hardware side, there are some
fairly cheap gadgets like the RD TC66 USB Tester. This looks a lot like the 
cheapy USB testers of old, showing you voltage and currently, but it also does
protocol detection to tell you what the cable and charger support. This lets
you know exactly what power is going into or out of your phone or laptop.

Up from there is the Treedix USB Cable Tester. This is just to test cables. 
Plug both ends of the cable you're curious about into it (and it can be just about any cable,
USB-C, USB-B, Thunderbolt, Lightning), and the big display tells you exactly what
pins that cable has, what protocols it's capable of, if the cable has an e-Marker
chip, and if the chip matches spec or is wired incorrectly.  Think of it as the 
really smart version of the simple continuity USB cable testers you may've seen several
years ago. 

And if you don't want to spend any money, there's some apps.  On the Mac, 
there's the very good WhatCable app that gives you much of the same info as the RD TC66
tester, at least as MacOS sees it. On Linux, there is the `lsucpd` commandline tool
and in general the kernel is very inspectable by poking around in `/sys/class/typec/`
virtual filesystem. 

Links to all these things in the show notes of course.

### CircuitPython audio improvements (Tod #2)

There's been some neat advances in audio-related stuff in CircuitPython lately. 

- `sdioio` on RP2040/RP2350 for high-speed connection to SD cards
- CIRCUITPY writeable `storage.{disable,enable}_usb_drive()` in `code.py`
- USB host fixes
- `audioi2sin` for audio INPUT
- `audiodelays`, `audiofilters`, and `audiofreeverb` on RP2040
- `audiospeed.SpeedChanger` & `audiospeed.Resampler`
- `audiodelays.GranularPitchShift`
- `audiofilewriter`

### AP30 Music Boy (Paul #2)

Next up I have the AP30 Music Boy, which may jsut be the most adorable retro gadget I’ve seen in some time. You’ll notice that it’s called the “Music Boy” and that’s definitely on purpose as this MP3 player looks like a Gameboy, from the screen to the buttons. It’s pretty small as it sits in the palm of your hand and features 2.0” IPS screen. It’s Hi-Res Audio certified and they really push how high quality the music is supposed to sound. It feature’s a 3.5mm and 4.4mm headphone jacks as well as Bluetooth 5.3 and you can install a microSD card to store your music.

You can stick it in your pocket, hang it on a lanyard, or it comes with a magnetic case to stick to the back of your phone. You can also use it as USB DAC on your computer to punch up the sound quality.

### Awesome self-hosted (Tod #3)

The [awesome-selfhosted](https://github.com/awesome-selfhosted/awesome-selfhosted)
list is another "awesome" list, but this time for free, open-source alternatives
to all the cloud-based subscription services we're starting to drown in.
I've been increasingly concerned that we're just becoming tenant-farmers on our
own information, seemingly barely owning anything we create or enjoy, instead
paying increasingly higher monthly fees just to live our lives. And now AI eats up
all our creative output and regurgitates it back to us for a fee. I'm a little worried!
The `awesome-selfhosted` might have some solutions for us.

It has hundreds of network services and web applications that accomplish most of what
we use cloud services for.  Of course it has links to how to run your own gmail-like
mail server, web servers, or git repositories, but it also has links to CMS document 
systems for small businesses, mapping systems like Google Maps, URL shorteners like bitly, 
backup systems like Backblaze, and video conferencing like Google Meet. 

Most all of these are bundled up into Docker-like containers so you can run multiple
on a single physical box. Or you could even run them on a remote virtual machine from
Digital Ocean or Linode.  Sure, it's a subscription cloud service, but it's only one
fee instead of many. 

Or find an old laptop or PC and put Linux and Docker on it and some of these services.
Most don't need much CPU. One of the nice things about having your data locally
is it will often feel faster when using it.  And if you are interested in tuning an AI, 
having a local one that pokes around your local data is much faster and cheaper and
secure than doing a bunch of remote API calls to a corporation you barely trust. 

I run several local servers already for things but this list has got me thinking
about moving more of my live to be directly under my control. And it might be fun!

- [awesome-selfhosted](https://github.com/awesome-selfhosted/awesome-selfhosted)

### Headphones and more headphones (Paul #3)

Paul's last two are headphones related. The first has been in the news for a bit, but was still new to me. Are you in the Android or Linux ecosystems and ever been jealous of the functionality Apple's AirPods have? Well, thanks to a project called [LibrePods](https://github.com/librepods-org/librepods), you can now use AirPod like features on Linux and Android.

The developers used Wireshark to reverse engineer Apple's proprietary protocols. You can use features like aut-connect, listening mode, battery status, and more.

* [LibrePods GitHub page](https://github.com/librepods-org/librepods)

And my last one is via Tom’s Hardware, which recently covered the Sony Head Tracker project by Nicholas Slattery on GitHub. If you have a high end pair of Sony’s noise canceling headphones, like the Sony WH-1000XM5’s, you’re in luck. 

These headphones have support for spatial audio, meaning they have sensors including gryoscopes and acceleromoters for head tracking. Mr. Slattery has reverse engineered the sensors firmware to allow you to use headtracking in games on macOS and Windows.  Once you have everything setup, you put your Sony headphones on and the in game camera will respond to your head movements - which is so cool.

Check out the article to see the list of headphones that are compatible and for more details on how it works.

* [Tom’s Hardware coverate of Sony Head Tracker](https://www.tomshardware.com/video-games/pc-gaming/you-can-now-use-your-sony-headphones-as-a-real-time-head-tracker-for-race-and-flight-simulators-on-pc-several-hundred-games-already-supported-enthusiast-creates-open-source-app-that-translates-live-sensor-data-into-in-game-camera-controls)
* [Sony Head Tracker on GitHub](https://github.com/NicholasSlattery/sony-head-tracker)
